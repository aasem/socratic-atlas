Arranged by when Plato wrote them, the pattern is clear. The Socrates who only asks belongs to the early group. Plato then gives him doctrines, has a young Socrates refuted by Parmenides, silences him while a visitor from Elea conducts the inquiry, and finally removes him entirely from the Laws, which is a work of legislation. The corpus itself does not stay in the armchair.

The Story the Dialogues Tell

Plato did not write the dialogues in the order of Socrates' life. The Parmenides, one of his later works, shows Socrates at about nineteen; the Euthyphro, an early work, shows him at seventy. Each dialogue has a dramatic date: the year in which the conversation is supposed to take place, worked out from the people present and the events mentioned. Arranged by those dates, the dialogues become one story of some fifty years, from a gifted young man refuted by Parmenides to an old man drinking hemlock, through a war that ruins most of the people he talks to. Several dates are approximate. Hollow markers are events from Athenian history.

Reading the Drama

The listeners act, and the story records what they did. Plato wrote after the events, for readers who knew them. Critias and Charmides, who discuss self-control, became leaders of the Thirty, a regime remembered for its killings. Alcibiades, who praises Socrates at dawn in the Symposium, was within months accused of sacrilege, then defected to Sparta. Meno betrayed his comrades in Persia. Nicias and Laches, the two experts on courage, died in battle. Polemarchus, host of the long night on justice, was executed by the Thirty. The arguments end in aporia; the lives end in history. In the drama itself, the gap between knowing and acting is not a theoretical puzzle but the tragedy of a generation.

Almost every conversation is cut off by life. A court filing in the Euthyphro, a lecture in the Hippias, the tutors in the Lysis, an indictment at the end of the Theaetetus, the ship from Delos in the Crito, the hemlock in the Phaedo. The dialogues are not set in an armchair. They are set in gymnasia, courts, prisons and festival nights during a war, and they end because something must be done. A reader who notices the frame cannot take them as a model of endless leisure.

The last month reorders the late dialogues. Placed by dramatic date, the Theaetetus, Euthyphro, Sophist and Statesman all fall in the days between Socrates' indictment and his trial. The silent Socrates of the Sophist is a defendant listening while a stranger investigates what separates a philosopher from a sophist, which is exactly what the jury will get wrong. The promised dialogue on the philosopher was never written. Socrates' death takes its place.

Socrates does not change; the city around him does. His method is the same at nineteen and seventy. What develops is the cost of it. The only question the dialogues settle without aporia is what this man will do when his principles meet the city's verdict, and the answer is given by an action, not a definition. Plato chose to transmit philosophy as the story of a life, told and retold by friends from memory, rather than as a treatise. For anyone writing in the dialogue form, the frame, the setting and the fates of the speakers are where action enters the text.

Plato's Warnings

Plato saw the danger of his own method. Seven passages speak to it directly. Each reference opens the passage in an English translation.

Apology 23c

Socrates admits the imitation problem

Wealthy young men follow him around and enjoy watching people examined, then go off and examine others themselves. The people they humiliate blame Socrates. He presents this as a side effect, not a goal.

Republic 537d–539d

No dialectic before thirty

Young people who taste refutation early treat it as a game, tear at arguments like puppies pulling at anything in reach, are refuted in turn, and end believing nothing they once held. Plato's own curriculum delays dialectic until after long training in mathematics and practical service. This is the most direct answer in the corpus to the fetish you describe.

Phaedo 89d–90e

The warning against misology

Someone who trusts arguments naively, then sees them collapse, comes to hate all argument, just as someone repeatedly betrayed comes to hate people. Socrates says to blame your own lack of skill, not reason itself. The danger runs both ways: credulity about arguments and contempt for them.

Euthydemus 271a–307c

Refutation for show is not philosophy

The dialogue stages two professional refuters whose tricks win applause and teach nothing, beside Socrates trying to turn a boy toward wisdom that is useful. Plato separates eristic (arguing to win) from philosophy (arguing to become better).

Parmenides 135c–d

Defining too early is a young man's error

Old Parmenides tells the young Socrates that he tried to define the beautiful, the just and the good before he had trained, and that his eagerness is admirable but premature.

Meno 97a–98a

Action does not wait for definition

True opinion guides action as well as knowledge does. Knowledge adds stability: it is opinion tied down by reasoning, so it does not run away like the statues of Daedalus.

Statesman 294a–c

No general rule fits every case

Law is like a stubborn, ignorant man who repeats the same order whatever the situation, because human affairs are too varied for any rule to cover. What rules cannot supply, judgment of measure and right timing (kairos) must.

Two Ways of Reading Plato

Reads Euthyphro through Republic I

The Uninitiated Reader

He meets a brilliant questioner who always wins and never commits. He learns that every confident person can be undone with three questions, and that saying "I know nothing" is a winning move. He has learned the technique without the reasons for it.

Plato wrote this reader into the text: the young imitators in the Apology, the puppies of Republic VII, the applauding crowd in the Euthydemus. The danger is real, and it comes from reading only part of the corpus.

Reads the corpus to the Laws

The Patient Reader

He sees refutation used to check false confidence before drastic action, then sees it bounded: by the trial and the prison, by warnings about misology and youthful dialectic, by the founder of the method being refuted, and by a final turn to measure, timing and law.

What he takes away is not "question everything forever" but a discipline for holding beliefs while acting on them, and for building institutions that outlast any one argument.

An Ethic of Inquiry

What the whole corpus teaches a person who must think and act at once.

Examine hardest the certainty you are about to act on drastically, especially against someone close to you.

Euthyphro

Act on your best true opinion; inquire so that it becomes stable and teachable, not so that you may finally be allowed to act.

Meno 97–98

Judge a teacher by whether his deeds match his words.

Laches 188c–e

When a valid-looking argument reaches a monstrous conclusion, distrust the argument before you obey it.

Hippias Minor

Never argue to win. Refutation that leaves no one better is a performance.

Euthydemus

When arguments fail you, blame your skill and keep reasoning. Neither credulity nor contempt.

Phaedo 89–90

Earn the right to abstraction through training and practical life; defining before you have lived is premature.

Parmenides 135; Republic VII

Rules generalize; situations do not. Cultivate judgment of measure and right timing.

Statesman 294

The good life is a mixture. A man who knows only perfect circles cannot find his way home.

Philebus 62a–b

Once you have decided, accept the cost of acting on it.

What Remains Unanswered

Some objections survive the whole corpus. Protagoras' Great Speech, that virtue is taught by the whole community the way a language is, is never refuted; Socrates changes the subject. It remains the strongest ancient statement of the view that traditions, not definitions, do the work of moral generalization.

Crito's charge that Socrates is abandoning his sons gets an answer about the city's laws, not about the sons. The household appears mostly as an interruption: the tutors who drag the boys home in the Lysis, Xanthippe sent away in the Phaedo.

And the early demand that you must define a concept before you can know any instance of it, which Peter Geach named the Socratic fallacy, is only implicitly withdrawn by Plato's later practice. It is never openly renounced, which is one reason readers who stop early inherit it.

On order and authorship. Stylometry gives good support for a late group (Sophist, Statesman, Philebus, Laws, with Timaeus and Critias). The order within the early group is conjecture. The authenticity of the Hippias Major is disputed. Cratylus, Menexenus, Timaeus, Critias and the Alcibiades I are omitted for length. How much of the early Socrates is the historical man and how much is Plato is the unresolved "Socratic problem"; Xenophon's very different, more practical Socrates is a useful counterweight.
